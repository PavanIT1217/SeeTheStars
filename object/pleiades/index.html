The Pleiades (M45)

Open star cluster

The Pleiades are a family of young, hot, blue stars that were born together. Six or seven are visible to the eye, but there are more than a thousand. The blue haze around them is a dust cloud they are passing through.

Names from a myth

In Greek myth the Pleiades were seven sisters, daughters of the giant Atlas and the sea nymph Pleione. Their parents are in the cluster too, as two of its brightest stars. The brightest of all is Alcyone, a blue giant at the centre of the little dipper shape.

📏

The distance puzzle

In the 1990s the Hipparcos satellite measured the Pleiades as about 390 light-years away, closer than other methods suggested. Scientists argued about it for years. Later measurements, including from the Gaia spacecraft, settled on about 444 light-years – showing why astronomers check results in more than one way.

🌱

A family that will scatter

The Pleiades stars were born together, but they won’t stay together forever. The gravity of passing stars and clouds slowly tugs them apart, and in a few hundred million years the family will have scattered. Our Sun was probably born in a cluster too, with long-lost sibling stars.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

Compared with our 4.6-billion-year-old Sun, these stars are babies – about 100 million years old. Dinosaurs were alive when they were born.

How we found out

  1. ~1600 BC

    The Nebra sky disc, found in Germany, may show the Pleiades – one of the oldest known images of the sky.

  2. 1610

    Galileo turns his telescope on them and counts dozens of stars.

  3. Around the world

    In Japan they’re Subaru (the car company’s logo shows them). In India they’re Krittika. For Māori people, their rising (Matariki) marks the New Year.

🤔 Something to wonder about

Why do so many cultures have a story about “seven sisters”?

Nobody is sure. One idea: the stories are extremely old, and one of the seven stars has since faded or moved closer to its neighbour.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 444 years ago – around the time Galileo first turned a telescope to the sky.

See it yourself

A tiny, misty “mini-dipper” of stars. Follow Orion’s belt up and to the right past the orange star Aldebaran.
