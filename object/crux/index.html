Crux – the Southern Cross

Constellation

Crux is the smallest constellation but one of the most famous. Its four bright stars form a cross that points towards the south celestial pole, guiding travellers in the Southern Hemisphere.

Take a closer look

🔭

Acrux is a team

To the eye, Acrux at the foot of the cross looks like one star. A small telescope splits it into two blue-white stars, and one of those is itself a close pair. Each of them is much hotter and brighter than our Sun.

💎

The Jewel Box

Next to the star Mimosa lies a young star cluster called the Jewel Box. In the 1830s John Herschel described it as a casket of coloured precious stones. Through a telescope you see dozens of blue-white stars with one striking orange-red star among them.

👉

Pointers and false cross

A bigger, dimmer “False Cross” nearby often fools beginners. To be sure you have the real one, find the two bright Pointer stars, Alpha and Beta Centauri, which point straight at it. Alpha Centauri is the nearest star system to us, about 4.4 light-years away.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

Right next to the cross is the Coalsack – a dark cloud of dust that blocks the stars behind it. Some Aboriginal Australians see it as the head of an emu in the sky.

How we found out

  1. Ancient times

    Visible from Greece and the Middle East thousands of years ago, but Earth’s wobble slowly moved it out of sight there.

  2. 1500s

    European sailors exploring the southern oceans use it to navigate.

  3. Today

    It appears on the flags of Australia, New Zealand, Brazil, Papua New Guinea and Samoa.

🤔 Something to wonder about

How do you find south using the Southern Cross?

Extend the long arm of the cross about four and a half times its length – that point is near the south celestial pole. Drop straight down to the horizon: that’s south.

See it yourself

Best from the Southern Hemisphere, and from southern India and the tropics in spring.
