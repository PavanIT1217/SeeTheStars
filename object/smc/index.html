Small Magellanic Cloud

Galaxy · Satellite of the Milky Way

The Small Magellanic Cloud is a dwarf galaxy orbiting the Milky Way. It is famous because studying its stars gave astronomers a way to measure the size of the universe.

Take a closer look

🧂

A Pinch of Heavy Elements

The Small Magellanic Cloud’s stars contain only about a fifth as much of the heavier elements – like oxygen and iron – as our Sun. That makes it a handy window into the past, because galaxies in the early universe were also short of these ingredients.

🌉

Pulled Like Taffy

The SMC is being tugged by both the Large Magellanic Cloud and the Milky Way. Their gravity has pulled out a long bridge of gas and stars – the Magellanic Bridge – that links the two Clouds, and a huge trail of gas that streams behind them across the sky.

🔭

Webb Finds Planet Nurseries

In the SMC’s busy star cluster NGC 346, the James Webb Space Telescope spotted young stars still wrapped in dusty discs that can build planets – even though the galaxy has so few heavy elements. Puzzlingly, these discs seem to last longer than similar ones in our own galaxy.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

Leavitt’s discovery, made by measuring tiny dots on glass photographic plates, turned out to be the key to measuring the entire universe.

How we found out

  1. 1908–1912

    Henrietta Swan Leavitt, studying photographic plates at Harvard, finds that Cepheid variable stars in the SMC that pulse more slowly are brighter.

  2. 1913

    Ejnar Hertzsprung uses her discovery to estimate the distance to the SMC.

  3. 1920s

    Edwin Hubble uses Leavitt’s law to measure the distance to Andromeda – and other galaxies.

🤔 Something to wonder about

How can you tell how far away a light is?

If you know how bright it really is, you can compare it to how bright it looks. Leavitt’s Cepheids are “standard candles” with a known real brightness.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 200,000 years ago – a while after the first modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared.
