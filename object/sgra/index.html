Sagittarius A*

Supermassive black hole

At the centre of our galaxy lies Sagittarius A* (say “A-star”), a supermassive black hole. Stars race around it in tight orbits, and by tracking them astronomers proved it’s there.

Take a closer look

🪐

Heavy but compact

Sagittarius A* has the mass of about 4 million Suns, yet its event horizon is only around 25 million km across. That would fit well inside the orbit of Mercury. If you shrank the Milky Way to the size of a football field, the black hole would be smaller than a grain of sand.

🍽️

A picky eater

Compared with many giant black holes, Sagittarius A* is surprisingly quiet, because very little gas falls into it. Even so, it flickers with small flares in X-rays and infrared light, often about once a day. Astronomers watch these flares to learn how black holes feed.

🧪

Testing Einstein

When the star S2 swings closest to the black hole, its light is stretched by gravity, exactly as Einstein’s theory predicts. Its orbit also slowly turns like a spirograph pattern, another of Einstein’s predictions. Scientists confirmed both effects in 2018 and 2020.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

A star called S2 orbits it every 16 years, reaching speeds of over 7,000 km per second.

How we found out

  1. 1974

    Bruce Balick and Robert Brown discover a compact radio source at the galactic centre.

  2. 1990s–2010s

    Teams led by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez track stars orbiting an invisible heavy object.

  3. 2020

    Genzel and Ghez share the Nobel Prize in Physics (with Roger Penrose).

  4. 2022

    The Event Horizon Telescope releases the first image of Sagittarius A*.

🤔 Something to wonder about

Will Sagittarius A* swallow the Milky Way?

No. It is enormous, but tiny compared to the galaxy. Stars far from it orbit safely, just as planets orbit the Sun without falling in.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 26,700 years ago – a while after people painted some of the oldest known cave art.

See it yourself

Point towards the “teapot” of Sagittarius, where the Milky Way is brightest. The centre is hidden behind dust.
