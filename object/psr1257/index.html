PSR B1257+12 planets

Pulsar planets · The very first exoplanets

The first planets ever confirmed outside our Solar System were found in a very strange place: orbiting a pulsar, the spinning leftover core of an exploded star. Nobody expected planets there!

Take a closer look

🌀

A spinning city-sized star

The pulsar at the centre of this system is the crushed core of a giant star. It is only about the size of a city, yet it weighs more than our Sun. It spins around about 160 times every second, sweeping beams of radio waves across space like a lighthouse.

🌑

Dark, dangerous skies

Standing on one of these planets would be nothing like Earth. There would be no warm sunshine, because the pulsar gives off very little visible light. Instead, the planets are blasted by powerful radiation and a fierce wind of tiny particles, making them very unlikely homes for life.

👻

Spooky names

In a public naming contest in 2015, this pulsar and its planets got suitably spooky names. The pulsar is Lich, an undead creature from fantasy stories, and its three planets are Draugr, Poltergeist and Phobetor – names from myths and ghost tales.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

One of the planets is only about twice the mass of our Moon – among the smallest planets known.

How we found out

  1. 1990

    Aleksander Wolszczan discovers the pulsar with the giant Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico.

  2. 1992

    Wolszczan and Dale Frail notice its super-regular pulses arriving a little early, then a little late – the tug of two orbiting planets.

  3. 1994

    A third, smaller planet is confirmed.

🤔 Something to wonder about

How could planets survive a star exploding?

Maybe they didn’t – they might have formed afterwards, from a disc of debris left around the pulsar.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 2,300 years ago – around the time Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth.

See it yourself

Only with radio telescopes. But you can point to where it is!
