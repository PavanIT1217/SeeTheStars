Vela Pulsar

Pulsar · Neutron star

The Vela Pulsar is the collapsed core of a star that exploded about 11,000 years ago. It is one of the brightest sources of gamma rays in the sky, and sometimes it suddenly spins a little faster – a “glitch”.

🔭 Find it in the sky

Take a closer look

🌕

A giant sky ghost

The cloud left by Vela’s explosion is enormous on our sky – about 16 full Moons wide. It is far too faint to see by eye, but long-exposure photos show a huge web of glowing threads. The pulsar sits inside it, slowly drifting through the wreckage.

🚒

The firehose jet

The Chandra X-ray telescope filmed a jet of particles shooting out of the Vela Pulsar. Over months it whips and wriggles from side to side, like a firehose that someone has let go of. Watching it helps scientists understand how pulsars fling energy into space.

🔦

Faint flashes of light

Although Vela is bright in radio waves and gamma rays, its visible light is incredibly faint. In 1977 astronomers in Australia finally caught it flashing in visible light, 11 times a second.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

When its supernova exploded, it would have been extremely bright in the skies of our ancestors.

How we found out

  1. 1968

    Australian astronomers at the Molonglo radio telescope discover it inside the Vela supernova remnant.

  2. 1968

    Together with the Crab Pulsar, it proves that pulsars are born in supernova explosions.

  3. Since then

    Scientists study its glitches to learn about the weird matter inside neutron stars.

🤔 Something to wonder about

Why would a spinning star suddenly speed up?

Scientists think the neutron star’s interior is partly a “superfluid” that occasionally transfers spin to the crust all at once.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 950 years ago – around the time astronomers in China watched the Crab supernova explode.

See it yourself

Southern sky, in the constellation Vela (the Sails). Radio and gamma-ray telescopes only.
