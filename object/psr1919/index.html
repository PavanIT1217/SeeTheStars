PSR B1919+21 – the first pulsar

Pulsar · Neutron star

This was the first pulsar ever found: a spinning neutron star that sweeps a beam of radio waves across Earth like a lighthouse. Its pulses are so regular that at first scientists wondered whether they were an alien signal.

🔨

A telescope built by hand

The radio telescope that found this pulsar was not a dish but a field of wooden posts and wire, covering more ground than two football pitches. Jocelyn Bell Burnell and other students helped build it. It printed its signals as wiggly lines on long rolls of paper, which she checked by hand.

🏮

When beams cross Earth

A pulsar’s beams sweep around as it spins, like a lighthouse. We only see one if a beam happens to cross Earth, so many pulsars are out there that we can never detect. Astronomers have now found more than 3,000.

⏱️

Clocks across the galaxy

Since 1967, pulsars have become some of the best clocks in the universe. In 1992 the first planets beyond the Solar System were found orbiting a pulsar. In 2023, teams timing dozens of pulsars found evidence of a gentle background “hum” of gravitational waves rippling through space.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

A chart of this pulsar’s signals became the famous cover of a 1979 rock album, Unknown Pleasures by Joy Division.

How we found out

  1. 1967

    Jocelyn Bell Burnell, a PhD student at Cambridge, spots a strange “bit of scruff” in her radio telescope data. It pulses every 1.337 seconds.

  2. 1967

    Half-jokingly, the signal is nicknamed “LGM-1” – Little Green Men 1.

  3. 1974

    The Nobel Prize for the discovery goes to her supervisor Antony Hewish (and Martin Ryle), not to her – a decision many people consider unfair.

  4. 2018

    Bell Burnell wins a $3 million Breakthrough Prize and donates it all to help students from under-represented groups become physicists.

🤔 Something to wonder about

How can something the size of a city spin once a second?

When a giant star collapses, it spins faster – like an ice skater pulling in their arms – just much, much more extreme.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 2,300 years ago – around the time Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth.

See it yourself

Only with radio telescopes. But you can point to where it is!
