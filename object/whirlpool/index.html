Whirlpool Galaxy (M51)

Galaxy · Interacting spiral

The Whirlpool Galaxy is a classic spiral seen face-on, tugged by a smaller companion galaxy. Their gravitational dance has sharpened its spiral arms and triggered the birth of many new stars.

Take a closer look

🎨

Painted Spiral Arms

Each arm of the Whirlpool is threaded with dark lanes of dust, sprinkled with blue clusters of young stars and dotted with pink clouds of glowing hydrogen. The pink patches are nurseries, lit up by newborn stars switching on inside them.

🌠

A View With a Neighbour

From a planet inside the Whirlpool, the companion galaxy NGC 5195 would hang in the sky as a large hazy glow. The nearby spiral arm would be crowded with bright young stars and glowing clouds, much busier than the night sky we see from Earth.

💥

Supernova Hotspot

Astronomers have spotted three supernovae – exploding stars – in the Whirlpool since 1994, and amateur astronomers helped discover some of them. Catching stars in the act of dying lets scientists check what kinds of stars blow up and how.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

Before anyone knew what galaxies were, Lord Rosse’s drawing of the Whirlpool showed the world that some “nebulae” had a spiral shape.

How we found out

  1. 1773

    Charles Messier discovers it.

  2. 1845

    Lord Rosse, using a giant telescope in Ireland called the “Leviathan”, sees and draws its spiral arms – the first time a spiral structure was recognised.

  3. 2005

    Hubble releases one of its most famous images of the galaxy.

🤔 Something to wonder about

Why do galaxies have spiral arms?

The arms are like traffic jams of stars and gas: regions where material piles up as it orbits, causing bursts of new, bright blue stars.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 30 million years ago.

See it yourself
