Cassiopeia

Constellation · The Queen

Cassiopeia is a bright “W” of five stars on the opposite side of Polaris from the Big Dipper. In Greek myth she was a vain queen who boasted about her beauty.

👑

The queen’s punishment

In Greek myth Cassiopeia’s boasting angered the sea god Poseidon, who sent a monster to attack her kingdom. Her daughter Andromeda was chained to a rock until the hero Perseus rescued her. As punishment, the queen circles the pole on her throne, spending half the night upside down.

💥

Cassiopeia A up close

Cassiopeia A is one of the youngest remains of an exploded star in our galaxy. Its light reached Earth around 1680, yet nobody clearly recorded it – thick dust probably hid the flash. X-ray telescopes show clouds of silicon, sulphur and iron flung out from the star’s insides.

🧭

Hop to Andromeda

The deeper “V” of Cassiopeia’s W points roughly towards the Andromeda Galaxy. On a dark night it looks like a faint oval smudge to the naked eye. Its light set off about 2.5 million years ago, making it one of the most distant things you can see without a telescope.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

Tycho’s supernova was so bright that it could be seen in daylight.

How we found out

  1. 1572

    Tycho Brahe sees a brilliant “new star” in Cassiopeia – a supernova. It shows that the heavens are not unchanging, a shocking idea at the time.

  2. 1947

    Radio astronomers detect Cassiopeia A, the remains of another exploded star and one of the strongest radio sources in the sky.

🤔 Something to wonder about

How can you use Cassiopeia to find the North Star?

Polaris sits roughly halfway between Cassiopeia and the Big Dipper. When one is low, the other is high.

See it yourself

Northern Hemisphere: look for a big “W” in the north. It never sets for people north of about 35° latitude.
