Triangulum Galaxy (M33)

Galaxy · Spiral

The Triangulum Galaxy is the third-largest galaxy in our Local Group, after Andromeda and the Milky Way. It is a beautiful, loosely wound spiral, full of pink star-forming regions.

🥚

No Bulge in the Middle

Unlike the Milky Way and Andromeda, Triangulum has no big bright bulge at its centre, and astronomers have found no sign of a giant black hole there. Instead it is rich in gas, which it is busily turning into fresh new stars along its loose, patchy arms.

🏭

Inside a Star Factory

Its star factory, NGC 604, is a glowing cloud so wide that light takes well over a thousand years to cross it – dozens of times wider than the Orion Nebula. More than 200 hot, blue, heavyweight stars blaze inside it, carving out huge bubbles in the gas.

📻

Watching a Galaxy Turn

By tracking tiny radio beacons in its gas clouds over several years, astronomers measured Triangulum actually turning – and sliding slowly across our sky. It was one of the first times anyone measured a spiral galaxy’s sideways motion directly, a bit like timing a carousel from millions of light-years away.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

Under perfectly dark skies, some people with excellent eyes can see it without a telescope – one of the most distant things a human eye can see.

How we found out

  1. Before 1654

    Giovanni Battista Hodierna probably observes it first.

  2. 1764

    Charles Messier catalogues it as M33.

  3. 2019

    Hubble releases a huge, detailed mosaic of the galaxy.

🤔 Something to wonder about

What is the “Local Group”?

Our galaxy’s neighbourhood: the Milky Way, Andromeda, Triangulum and dozens of smaller dwarf galaxies, held together by gravity.

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 2.7 million years ago – around the time early human ancestors made the first stone tools.
