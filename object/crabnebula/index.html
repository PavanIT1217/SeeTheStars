Crab Nebula (M1)

Supernova remnant

The Crab Nebula is the wreckage of a star that exploded as a supernova. The explosion was seen on Earth in 1054. At its centre spins a pulsar, the star’s collapsed core.

Take a closer look

🧶

Threads of star stuff

The orange-red strands that criss-cross the Crab are the shredded outer layers of the star that blew up. They contain hydrogen, helium, oxygen and other elements made inside it. The whole cloud is now about 11 light-years across and still growing.

🌀

Powered by a spinning heart

The ghostly blue glow filling the nebula comes from tiny particles racing through magnetic fields, flung out by the pulsar at the centre. As the pulsar slowly loses spin, it pumps that energy into the cloud. Without it, the Crab would have faded long ago.

📏

A measuring stick for X-rays

The Crab is one of the brightest and steadiest X-ray sources in the sky. Because of this, astronomers have often used it to calibrate their instruments. They even measure the brightness of other X-ray sources in “Crabs” and “milliCrabs”.

✨ Mind-blowing fact

The cloud is still expanding at about 1,500 km per second.

How we found out

  1. 1054

    Chinese astronomers record a “guest star” bright enough to see in daylight for 23 days.

  2. 1758

    Charles Messier mistakes it for a comet. To avoid more mix-ups he starts his famous catalogue – and it becomes M1.

  3. 1840s

    Lord Rosse draws it and names it the Crab.

  4. 1968

    Astronomers discover the pulsar at its centre.

🤔 Something to wonder about

Where do atoms like gold and iron come from?

Many heavy elements were made in exploding and colliding stars and spread through space. Some ended up in Earth – and in you!

⏳ Light is a time machine

The light reaching you now set off about 6,500 years ago – a while before people first invented writing.

See it yourself

A faint smudge in a small telescope, near the tip of Taurus’ horn.
